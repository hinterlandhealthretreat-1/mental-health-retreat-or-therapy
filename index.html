Mental Health Retreat, Hospital or Weekly Therapy: Which One Do You Need?

A plain-English guide for Australians weighing up their options · 8 minute read

When you're struggling, it's hard to know what kind of help you actually need. Do you book a psychologist, look at a hospital program, or take time out at a mental health retreat? If you're not sure what a retreat even involves, this explainer on what a residential mental health retreat involves is a good place to start. Below, we compare all three options side by side, so you can work out which one fits where you are right now.

The short version

  • Weekly therapy suits most people with mild to moderate stress, anxiety or low mood.
  • A mental health retreat suits people who feel stuck despite therapy and need time away to focus on recovery.
  • Hospital care is for people who are unwell enough to need 24-hour medical support, or who are at risk.

What is the main difference between a retreat, a hospital and therapy?

The main difference is how much support you get and how much of your normal life you step away from. Weekly therapy fits around your life. A retreat takes you out of it for a while. A hospital gives you round-the-clock medical care. Small residential retreats explain this well on their website, including who their programs are and aren't designed for.

None of these is better than the others. They're built for different needs, and many people move between them over time. Someone might start with a psychologist, spend a few weeks at a mental health retreat in Australia when things get harder, and then go back to weekly sessions afterwards.

When is weekly therapy enough?

Weekly therapy is usually enough when you can still manage work, home and relationships, even if it's hard. It's the most common starting point, and for many people it's all they need.

How it works

You see a psychologist, counsellor or other mental health professional for about an hour, usually every week or two. Sessions can be in person or by video. With a Mental Health Treatment Plan from your GP, Medicare covers part of the cost of up to 10 individual sessions a year.

Good fit if: you're dealing with stress, anxiety, low mood or a life change, and you can keep up with daily life.

When should you consider a mental health retreat?

Consider a mental health retreat when weekly therapy isn't giving you enough traction, or when daily life keeps getting in the way of your recovery. A retreat gives you time and space to focus fully on getting better.

How it works

You stay on site for several days or weeks. Your day usually includes therapy sessions, movement, healthy meals, rest and time outdoors. Good programs include planning for when you go home, so the benefits last.

Good fit if: you feel burnt out or stuck, you need distance from work or home pressure, and you're safe and stable enough to take part in a program.

Mental health retreats in Australia range from larger centres to small programs that take only a handful of guests. Many people look at a health retreat on the Sunshine Coast or elsewhere in Queensland because it's close to Brisbane and the quiet hinterland setting helps people switch off. Wherever you look, check that the team includes qualified psychologists, not only wellness staff.

When do you need hospital care?

You need hospital care when your symptoms are severe, you need close medical support, or you're at risk of harming yourself. Hospitals can manage medication changes, medical detox and situations a retreat can't safely handle.

How it works

Public hospitals have mental health units, and there are private psychiatric hospitals too. Admission to a private hospital is usually arranged through a psychiatrist. Private hospital cover may help with the cost, depending on your policy.

Good fit if: you're very unwell, you need 24-hour care, or you don't feel safe.

If you feel unsafe right now, don't wait. Call 000, go to your nearest emergency department, or call Lifeline on 13 11 14.

Is there a government funded mental health retreat option?

There's no government program that pays for a private mental health retreat. What you can get is free public support, including short-stay residential services in some states, free walk-in Medicare Mental Health Centres, and Medicare rebates for therapy.

If you're looking for a government funded mental health retreat, the best first step is your GP or your state's mental health access line. They can tell you what public options are near you and whether you'd qualify.

How do you decide what's right for you?

Decide by being honest about how you're coping day to day, and by getting a professional opinion. These questions can help:

  1. How long have I been feeling this way?
  2. Am I still managing work, home and relationships?
  3. Has regular therapy helped, or do I feel stuck?
  4. Do I need time away from my usual surroundings to get better?
  5. Do I feel safe?

Take your answers to your GP. They can help you choose the right level of care and refer you where you need to go.

Frequently asked questions

Is a mental health retreat the same as rehab?

Not always. Some retreats focus on stress, burnout and mood. Others offer addiction support. Medical detox usually needs a hospital or specialist service.

Can I go to a mental health retreat instead of hospital?

Only if you're safe and stable enough. A retreat isn't a replacement for hospital care when you need 24-hour medical support.

How long does a mental health retreat last?

Most programs run from one to four weeks. Some offer shorter stays of a few days.

Do I need a referral for a mental health retreat?

Most private retreats don't require one, but talking to your GP first is always a good idea.

Will I still need therapy after a retreat?

Usually, yes. Follow-up sessions help you keep using what you learned and make the benefits last.

Final thoughts

There's no wrong door when it comes to getting help. Weekly therapy, a mental health retreat and hospital care each have their place, and the right one depends on where you are right now. Start with an honest chat with your GP, and remember that asking for support is a strong first step.

For support any time, visit Beyond Blue or call Lifeline on 13 11 14. In an emergency, call 000.
